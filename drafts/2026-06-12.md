## The Move
### Mortgage Payoff vs. Investing at 6.52%: The Math Most People Get Wrong

At a 6.52% mortgage rate, extra principal payments deliver a guaranteed, risk-free nominal return of 6.52%. That's the starting number. The question is whether a 60/40 portfolio can beat it after accounting for taxes, risk, and time horizon.

**Step 1: The after-tax cost of the mortgage**

The Tax Cuts and Jobs Act (TCJA) raised the standard deduction to $30,000 for married filers in 2026 (indexed from the 2017 baseline). The mortgage interest deduction under Internal Revenue Service (Internal Revenue Code) §163(h) only delivers value if itemized deductions exceed that threshold. For most dual-income households with a standard-sized mortgage, itemizing no longer pencils out. Result: the mortgage rate is its own after-tax hurdle. The guaranteed return on extra principal payments = **6.52% nominal, after-tax, risk-free.**

For the rare reader still itemizing — say, a $1.5M mortgage with $80K+ in annual interest plus state taxes — the after-tax cost drops. At a 37% marginal rate, effective mortgage cost ≈ 6.52% × (1 − 0.37) = **4.11%.** That changes the calculus entirely, but it applies to a shrinking minority.

**Step 2: What the 60/40 portfolio actually needs to return**

For non-itemizers, the portfolio must beat 6.52% *after tax* on investment gains. Equity gains in a taxable brokerage are subject to Long-Term Capital Gains (LTCG) rates (15–20% for most high-income readers) plus the 3.8% Net Investment Income Tax (NIIT) for those above $250K modified Adjusted Gross Income (AGI) married filing jointly. Blended effective tax drag on a 60/40 portfolio — assuming mostly long-term equity gains and ordinary-income bond interest — runs approximately 22–28% for a reader in the 32–37% bracket.

Required *pre-tax* portfolio return to match the mortgage payoff:
- **6.52% ÷ (1 − 0.25 blended rate) = 8.69% nominal**

The long-run nominal return for a 60/40 portfolio (60% equities at ~9.5% historical nominal, 40% bonds at ~4.5%) is approximately **7.5%**. That falls short of 8.69%.

For an 80/20 portfolio, the expected nominal return is closer to **8.2%** — still short, and with substantially more volatility.

**Step 3: The $50K, 10-year comparison**

*Scenario A: $50,000 applied as extra principal on a 6.52% mortgage*
- Guaranteed interest avoided over 10 years ≈ $50,000 × [(1.0652)^10 − 1] in simple compounding terms is misleading for amortizing debt; the accurate figure for interest saved on a lump-sum principal paydown with 20 years remaining is approximately **$47,200** in future interest avoided (using a standard amortization model at 6.52%, $50K prepayment, 20-year remaining term). That's real, after-tax dollars not owed.
- Equivalent guaranteed return: **6.52% nominal, risk-free, after-tax** for non-itemizers.

*Scenario B: $50,000 invested in a 60/40 taxable brokerage (e.g., 60% Vanguard Total Stock Market Index Fund ETF (VTI) at 0.03% expense ratio, 40% Vanguard Total Bond Market Index Fund ETF (BND) at 0.03%)*
- Assumed nominal gross return: 7.5% annualized
- Terminal gross value at 10 years: $50,000 × (1.075)^10 = **$101,366**
- Estimated tax liability at liquidation (blended 25% effective rate on gains): ($101,366 − $50,000) × 0.25 = **$12,842**
- **Terminal after-tax value: $88,524**
- Net after-tax gain: $38,524

Compare to Scenario A: $47,200 in avoided interest (which flows directly to net worth — no tax event). **Mortgage payoff wins by ~$8,700 on this 10-year horizon**, assuming 7.5% gross portfolio returns.

For the portfolio to match, it needs to compound at roughly **8.5–8.7% gross** over 10 years — achievable historically in 100% equity allocations, not in a 60/40 structure.

**When investing wins anyway**

Three conditions shift the math back toward the brokerage:
1. **Tax-advantaged space is available.** If the $50K can go into a Roth Individual Retirement Account (IRA) or mega-backdoor Roth 401(k), the tax drag disappears. Required gross return drops to 6.52% — and a 60/40 at 7.5% gross now wins. Max those accounts before directing extra cash to principal.
2. **Long horizon (15+ years).** Equity volatility averages out; a 100% equity allocation historically clears 8.69% over 15-year periods more often than not.
3. **Mortgage rate is below ~5.5%.** At that level, even a taxable 60/40 portfolio at 7.5% gross clears the after-tax hurdle comfortably.

**The bottom line**

At 6.52% with a taxable brokerage and a 60/40 allocation, paying down the mortgage is the higher expected after-tax return over a 10-year horizon — not because of risk aversion, but because the arithmetic says so. The standard advice to "always invest over paying down a low-rate mortgage" was correct at 3%. It is not correct at 6.52% for most non-itemizing households. Fill tax-advantaged accounts first; then the mortgage wins the marginal dollar.
