## The Move
### I-Bonds vs. Short Treasuries: The Break-Even Hold Is Longer Than You Think

The current Series I Savings Bond composite rate is **4.28%** (fixed component: 1.20%; inflation component derived from the prior Consumer Price Index (CPI) semi-annual change annualized at approximately 3.08%). Against the 3-month Treasury yield of **3.83%**, the headline spread looks like 45 basis points in the I-Bond's favor. The actual after-tax arithmetic is messier — and the break-even hold period is the number that determines which instrument belongs in your cash sleeve.

**Step 1: State-tax advantage of both instruments**

Both Series I Savings Bonds and Treasuries are exempt from state and local income tax under 31 U.S.C. § 3124. That's a wash — though it does mean both beat a High-Yield Savings Account (HYSA) or CD for anyone in a high-tax state like California (13.3% top marginal) or New York City (combined ~14.8%).

**Step 2: Federal tax treatment diverges**

The 3-month Treasury pays interest at maturity and is federally taxable in the year received — no deferral. I-Bond interest accrues but is federally taxable only at redemption (or at maturity in 30 years), giving you full tax deferral under IRC § 454. For a household in the 32% federal bracket, that deferral has real value. For a household in the 22% bracket, it matters less.

**Step 3: The early-redemption penalty**

Redeem an I-Bond before 5 years and you forfeit the last 3 months of interest. Redeem before 12 months: you can't at all. So the relevant holding periods are:
- Under 12 months: I-Bond is completely illiquid. Treasury wins by default.
- 12–60 months: I-Bond is accessible but subject to the 3-month forfeiture.
- 60+ months: Full I-Bond rate, no penalty.

**Step 4: Net after-tax yield comparison at the 12-month minimum hold**

Assume a 32% federal bracket, 0% state tax (since both are state-exempt), and a $10,000 position.

*3-month Treasury, rolled four times over 12 months:*
Gross yield: 3.83% annualized. After federal tax: 3.83% × (1 − 0.32) = **2.60% net**.
Terminal value: $10,000 × 1.0260 = **$10,260**.

*I-Bond held exactly 12 months (minimum), then redeemed:*
Gross composite: 4.28% (held as fixed for this calculation; in practice the inflation component resets every 6 months). But the 3-month penalty forfeits the last quarter's accrual.
Effective gross yield after penalty: 4.28% × (9/12) = **3.21%**.
That 3.21% is deferred until redemption, so you owe federal tax at redemption: 3.21% × (1 − 0.32) = **2.18% net**.
Terminal value: $10,000 × 1.0218 = **$10,218**.

At the 12-month minimum, the T-bill wins by roughly **$42 per $10,000** — even though the gross I-Bond rate is higher. The 3-month penalty erases the rate advantage and then some.

**Step 5: Break-even hold period**

The penalty becomes less costly as a fraction of total accrued interest the longer you hold. The crossover happens around **18–20 months** of actual holding, assuming the current rate differential holds and rolling 3-month Treasuries continue yielding ~3.83%.

At 18 months:
- T-bill (rolled): 3.83% × 1.5 × (1 − 0.32) ≈ **3.89% cumulative net**.
- I-Bond (18 months held, 3-month penalty): 4.28% × (15/12) = 5.35% gross effective; net after 32% tax at redemption: **3.64% cumulative net**. Still below.

At 24 months:
- T-bill: 3.83% × 2 × (1 − 0.32) ≈ **5.21% cumulative net**.
- I-Bond (24 months, penalty still applies): 4.28% × (21/12) = 7.49% gross effective; net: **5.09% cumulative net**. Nearly equal.

At 36 months:
- T-bill: 3.83% × 3 × (1 − 0.32) ≈ **7.81% cumulative net**.
- I-Bond (36 months, penalty): 4.28% × (33/12) = 11.77% gross effective; net: **8.00% cumulative net**. **I-Bond wins** by ~19 basis points cumulative, or roughly $19 per $10,000.

True crossover is approximately **30–32 months** under current rates. Past 60 months (no penalty), the I-Bond's deferral advantage and higher gross rate compounds more cleanly.

**The decision rule:**
- Cash sleeve you might need in under 18 months → 3-month Treasury. Better liquidity, better net return at that horizon.
- Cash sleeve for 3–5 years, in a high federal bracket, with inflation risk you want hedged → I-Bond earns the allocation. The $10,000/person annual cap limits the position size; a dual-income household can deploy $20,000/year.
- Horizon 5+ years, no inflation hedge needed → that money probably belongs in equities, not either of these instruments.

One wrinkle: if Core CPI (CPI) reaccelerates above its current 2.6% Year-over-Year (YoY) print, the I-Bond inflation component resets upward at each 6-month interval, pulling the break-even shorter. At 3.83% short-Treasury yields and 2.6% inflation, the spread is thin enough that the I-Bond's case rests almost entirely on the fixed 1.20% component and tax deferral — not on an inflation windfall.
