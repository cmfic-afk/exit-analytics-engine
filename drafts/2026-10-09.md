## The Move
### The 529-to-Roth Conversion Window: $35K That Most Families Are Leaving on the Table

Under Section 126 of the Setting Every Community Up for Retirement Enhancement Act (SECURE Act) 2.0 — codified at IRC §529(c)(3)(E) — you can roll funds from a 529 plan directly into a Roth Individual Retirement Account (IRA) for the beneficiary. The mechanics are tight, so the strategy only works if you engineer it in advance.

**The hard rules:**
- The 529 must have been open for at least 15 years.
- The beneficiary of the 529 must be the owner of the Roth IRA receiving the rollover.
- Annual rollovers are capped at the Roth IRA contribution limit for that year ($7,000 in 2026 for those under 50).
- Lifetime cap per beneficiary: $35,000.
- Contributions made (and earnings on those contributions) within the last 5 years are ineligible.
- The beneficiary must have earned income at least equal to the rollover amount in the year of conversion — same rule as direct Roth contributions.

**The terminal value math:**

$35,000 converted into a Roth IRA at age 25, invested at 7% real (roughly historical U.S. equity returns net of 2.4% CPI), held to age 65:

$$35,000 \times (1.07)^{40} = 35,000 \times 14.974 = \$524,090$$

That $524,090 comes out tax-free at retirement. No Required Minimum Distributions (RMDs). No income inclusion. No Medicare surcharge exposure.

For comparison, the same $35,000 left in the 529 and eventually withdrawn for non-qualified expenses would face ordinary income tax plus a 10% penalty on the earnings portion. At a 32% marginal rate, even a modest earnings portion would erase tens of thousands in value.

**The timeline problem — and how to pre-solve it:**

With a $7,000 annual ceiling, deploying the full $35,000 takes exactly five years of rollovers (5 × $7,000 = $35,000). The 15-year account age requirement is the binding constraint for most families, because it means the clock must start well before the child's college years.

Optimal setup: open a 529 for each child at or near birth. Fund it minimally if you're uncertain about education costs — even $1,000 starts the 15-year clock. If your child ends up with scholarships, attends a lower-cost school, or skips college entirely, the account is already aged into eligibility by the time they hit 25–27.

Families who intend to over-fund the 529 deliberately — contributing more than they expect to spend on education — can treat the excess as quasi-Roth space from the start. This is particularly effective for high earner not rich yet (HENRY) households that are phased out of direct Roth IRA contributions (2026 phase-out begins at $150,000 modified Adjusted Gross Income (AGI) for single filers, $236,000 for married filing jointly) and have already maxed their backdoor Roth options.

**The comparison that matters:**

Using the preferred terminal-value framing: assume a 32% tax rate at conversion and a 24% effective rate at retirement (approximating Required Minimum Distribution (RMD) bracket exposure for a traditional account).

- **529-to-Roth path:** $35,000 rolls in with no tax event (the 529 dollars were after-tax at contribution). Terminal value at 65: **$524,090 tax-free**.
- **Traditional IRA path (if eligible):** $35,000 pre-tax → $(35,000) × (1.07)^{40} × (1 − 0.24) = $524,090 × 0.76 = **$398,308 after tax**.
- **Taxable account path:** $35,000 after-tax, assuming Long-Term Capital Gains (LTCG) rate of 15% on gains: terminal value = $35,000 + ($524,090 − $35,000) × 0.85 = $35,000 + $415,576 = **$450,576 after tax**.

The Roth terminal value wins by $73,514 over the taxable path and $125,782 over the traditional path — on the same $35,000 of capital.

**The catches worth knowing:**

First, the 5-year contribution exclusion is easy to trip: if you make a large 529 contribution in your child's senior year of high school — a common move for state tax deductions — those dollars and their earnings are ineligible for rollover for five years, potentially past the window where the beneficiary has earned income. Contributions made in year 1–10 of the account are cleanest.

Second, the beneficiary must have earned income in the rollover year. A 25-year-old working a first job satisfies this easily. A stay-at-home adult child or full-time graduate student may not.

Third, the $35,000 lifetime cap is per beneficiary, not per account. Changing the 529 beneficiary to a sibling resets the cap for that sibling — a clean strategy if one child uses less education funding than another.

The planning decision is simple: if you have children under 8 and any reasonable expectation of college savings, open the 529 now even if you fund it lightly. The 15-year clock is the only resource that can't be purchased later.
