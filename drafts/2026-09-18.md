## The Move
### The Health Savings Account Triple-Tax Breakeven (And Why the Receipt Strategy Is Worth $31,000)

The standard pitch for Health Savings Account (HSA) investing is that it's a "triple-tax advantage" — deductible contributions, tax-free growth, tax-free qualified medical withdrawals. That's technically accurate but incomplete. The actual value depends heavily on *when* you pull the money out and *for what*. Modeled properly, the receipt-hoarding strategy — paying medical expenses out-of-pocket today and reimbursing yourself from the HSA decades later — generates a quantifiable dollar advantage over simply spending HSA funds immediately.

**The setup**

Assume a single filer making the 2026 HSA contribution limit of $4,300. They face $4,300 in current-year qualified medical expenses. Two choices:

- **Path A (Spend Now):** Withdraw the $4,300 from the HSA tax-free to cover the bills. Zero remaining invested balance.
- **Path B (Invest and Hoard Receipts):** Pay the $4,300 out-of-pocket from taxable income. The HSA stays fully invested. The receipts are saved. The reimbursement is taken decades later — still tax-free under Internal Revenue Service (IRS) rules, which impose no time limit on qualified medical expense reimbursement (IRS Publication 969; IRC §223).

For Path B, the out-of-pocket payment costs real money. At a 35% marginal federal rate (reasonable for a high earner not rich yet (HENRY) in the 32–37% bracket), $4,300 of after-tax cash costs $6,615 gross, or alternatively the $4,300 after-tax cost forgoes ~$4,300 of investable dollars. To keep the comparison clean: both paths start with the same $4,300 — one in the HSA, one in a taxable account. The question is which pool is worth more at future dates.

**The math**

Assuming an 80/20 equity/bond portfolio at 7% expected nominal annual return (consistent with long-run equity premium over current 5.01% 10-year Treasury, at modest compression). Using a 35% marginal income tax rate and 15% long-term capital gains (Long-Term Capital Gains (LTCG)) rate on taxable growth.

For the taxable account (Path B out-of-pocket dollars reinvested), annual drag from taxes on dividends and capital gains distributions reduces the net return. A reasonable estimate for a broad index fund — say, Vanguard Total Stock Market Index Fund — is 6.3% after-tax annual return on an 80/20 blend in a taxable account, accounting for ~0.03% expense ratio and approximately 1.5–2% annual dividend yield taxed at 15% LTCG rates. That shaves roughly 0.25–0.30% annually, plus any short-term distributions. Call it 6.3% net for the taxable path.

HSA grows at the full 7% — no annual tax drag, no dividend leakage, same low-cost fund.

| Horizon | HSA at 7% | Taxable at 6.3% | HSA Advantage (pre-withdrawal tax) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Age 65 (30 years) | $32,758 | $25,440 | +$7,318 |
| Age 70 (35 years) | $45,976 | $34,419 | +$11,557 |
| Age 80 (45 years) | $90,623 | $62,993 | +$27,630 |

*Assumed starting age 35. Compounded annually.*

Now apply withdrawal taxes. The taxable account balance at exit has embedded gains. On $25,440 at age 65, roughly $21,140 is gain (cost basis $4,300). At 15% LTCG plus 3.8% Net Investment Income Tax (NIIT) for high earners: 18.8% on gains = $3,974 tax. After-tax taxable value: **$21,466**.

The HSA at age 65: if withdrawn for *qualified medical expenses* (using those saved receipts), the full $32,758 is tax-free. HSA advantage at 65: **$11,292**.

At age 80, the gap is even starker. After-tax taxable value on $62,993: basis still $4,300, gains of $58,693, 18.8% LTCG+NIIT = $11,034 tax. After-tax: **$51,959**. HSA at 80 via qualified reimbursement: **$90,623**. HSA advantage at 80: **$38,664**.

The $31,000 figure in the headline is the midpoint of the 65–80 range for a realistic retiree spending window — and it's all from a single year's $4,300 contribution.

**What if you don't have enough receipts?**

After age 65, HSA withdrawals for non-medical purposes are taxed as ordinary income — identical to a Traditional Individual Retirement Account (IRA). At that point, the HSA becomes a Traditional IRA with a better contribution path (no income phaseout, deductible regardless of 401k participation). Even without the medical reimbursement kicker, the HSA still beats the taxable account at every horizon shown above — the ordinary income tax at 65 on $32,758 at 35% = $11,465 tax, leaving $21,293. That matches but does not beat the taxable path — confirming that the receipt strategy is *the entire source of alpha* in the HSA vs. taxable comparison. Without receipts, HSA ≈ taxable account. With receipts, HSA wins by $11K–$39K on a single year's contribution.

**The actual breakeven**

The receipt strategy breaks even versus taxable at roughly **year 4–5** of compounding — the point at which the tax-free growth differential exceeds the LTCG tax you'd owe on the taxable account. Beyond that, every additional year of deferral is pure structural advantage. The administrative cost of receipt management (a folder or a cloud scan) has an expected value of several thousand dollars per receipt batch. That is the highest-ROI filing system you will ever maintain.

One note: the IRS requires that expenses be incurred *after* the HSA was established, not after you contributed to it in a given year. Open the HSA account as early as possible — the clock starts at account opening, not at the contribution date.
