## The Move
### Roth Conversions: The 32% Bracket Is Where Deferral Stops Winning

Assumptions up front: $200,000 Traditional Individual Retirement Account (IRA) balance, age 55, retirement at 65, Required Minimum Distribution (RMDs) begin at 73 (per SECURE 2.0, IRC §401(a)(9)). That gives us 18 years to RMD onset and a 10-year accumulation window post-conversion. Risk-free discount rate: 4.47% (current 10-year Treasury yield). Projected equity return: 7.0% nominal (roughly consistent with long-run U.S. equity averages net of current valuations). Tax rates at RMD are assumed equal to conversion-year rates — a conservative assumption that actually understates the conversion case for accounts that grow large.

**The core mechanics:**

A Roth conversion means paying tax *now* on the converted amount, then letting the after-tax balance compound tax-free. The alternative — leave it in the Traditional IRA — defers that tax bill but compounds the future tax liability alongside the asset.

**Scenario A — 24% bracket**

Convert $200,000 today. Tax cost: $48,000. Net Roth balance starts at $152,000 (assuming tax paid from outside funds, which is the correct comparison — paying conversion tax from the IRA itself is structurally worse and changes the math significantly). That $152,000 grows at 7% for 18 years: **$152,000 × (1.07)^18 = $514,600**, fully tax-free.

Alternative: $200,000 stays in Traditional IRA, grows at 7% for 18 years = **$676,000**. RMDs at 73 are taxed at 24%. Present value of that tax liability, discounted at 4.47% over 18 years: the IRS will eventually collect roughly 24% of $676,000 = $162,240, discounted back = $162,240 / (1.0447)^18 = **~$73,200** in today's dollars.

Tax cost of converting today: $48,000. PV of future tax if you don't: ~$73,200. **Net advantage of converting: ~$25,200.** But wait — you're also giving up 18 years of investment return on the $48,000 tax payment. At 4.47% risk-free: $48,000 × (1.0447)^18 = ~$103,500 foregone. That flips the math. At 24%, deferral still wins by roughly $30,000 in opportunity-cost-adjusted terms.

**Scenario B — 32% bracket**

Same $200,000. Tax cost today: $64,000. Roth starts at $136,000 → grows to **$136,000 × (1.07)^18 = $459,800**.

Traditional IRA grows to $676,000. Future tax at 32%: $216,320. PV of that liability: $216,320 / (1.0447)^18 = **~$97,700**.

Opportunity cost of paying $64,000 now: $64,000 × (1.0447)^18 = **~$138,000**.

Net: PV of future tax ($97,700) vs. OC of paying now ($138,000). Deferral still edges ahead — but by only ~$40,300, and that gap collapses if your RMD-era bracket rises even modestly (say, Social Security + pension income) or if the account grows faster than 7%. At 32%, you are in the break-even zone.

**Scenario C — 35% bracket**

Tax cost today: $70,000. OC at 4.47% over 18 years: **~$151,000**.

Future tax on $676,000 at 35%: $236,600. PV: **~$107,000**.

Here the PV of future taxes ($107,000) is still below the OC of paying now ($151,000) — but the margin is thin, and critically, **the Tax Cuts and Jobs Act (TCJA) individual rate provisions sunset after 2025** (extended versions notwithstanding). If the 35% bracket reverts to 39.6%, the Traditional IRA holder faces a ~$267,000 tax bill at RMD onset. PV of that: ~$120,700 — now exceeding the $107,000 OC of converting at 35% today. **At 35% with TCJA sunset risk priced in, conversion wins.**

**The verdict:** The 4.47% risk-free rate raises the bar for conversion compared to a low-rate environment — every dollar of tax paid today has a real opportunity cost. At 24%, defer. At 32%, you're close enough that account growth trajectory and RMD-era income projections should decide it. At 35% with any plausible rate-increase scenario (sunset, bracket creep, large account growth), convert. The crossover is 32%.

One practical note: partial conversions to fill the top of a lower bracket — say, converting just enough to stay under the 32%/35% threshold — capture most of the upside without committing to the full opportunity cost calculation above.

## Closing

The Roth conversion debate is almost always framed as a tax-rate bet — but at 4.47% on the 10-year, it's equally a question of what it costs to write the Internal Revenue Service (IRS) a check today versus a larger one in 18 years, and right now that cost is real.
