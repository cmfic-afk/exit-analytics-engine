## The Move
### The After-Tax Yield Ranking That Just Shifted

Gross yield headlines are noise for anyone in a high bracket. Here's where four common income instruments actually land after the Internal Revenue Service (IRS) takes its cut, using a 32% federal marginal rate and a 5% average state rate (combined marginal 37% on ordinary income).

**Assumptions applied throughout:**
- Federal marginal rate: 32%
- State rate: 5% (ordinary income; Treasuries are state-exempt)
- Long-Term Capital Gains (LTCG) / qualified dividend rate: 15% federal + 5% state = 20% combined
- No Net Investment Income Tax (NIIT) applied (adds 3.8% on investment income above $200K single / $250K married; if you're subject to it, knock every number below down further — NIIT hits ordinary income and qualified dividends alike)
- Expense ratios reduce pre-tax yield before the tax calculation

---

**1. 10-Year Treasury at 4.47%**

Treasury interest is federally taxable, state-exempt (31 USC § 3124).

- Pre-tax yield: 4.47%
- Federal tax: 4.47% × 32% = 1.43%
- State tax: exempt
- **After-tax yield: 4.47% − 1.43% = 3.04%**

For a resident of a high-tax state, the state exemption is real money. A 5% state bite on 4.47% would cost another 0.22%, dropping the after-tax yield to 2.82%. The Treasury wins that comparison by 22 basis points over a fully-taxable instrument at the same gross rate.

---

**2. Top High-Yield Savings Account (HYSA) at ~4.10% Annual Percentage Yield (APY)**

Federally Insured Deposit Insurance Corporation (FDIC)-insured deposit interest: fully ordinary income at both federal and state levels.

- Pre-tax yield: 4.10%
- Federal tax: 4.10% × 32% = 1.31%
- State tax: 4.10% × 5% = 0.21%
- **After-tax yield: 4.10% − 1.31% − 0.21% = 2.58%**

The HYSA loses to the 10-year Treasury by 46 basis points after tax, despite being only 37 basis points lower in gross yield. The state-exemption gap is the entire story. If you're in a zero-income-tax state (TX, FL, NV, WA, etc.), the HYSA closes to within 22 basis points of the Treasury after tax — close enough that liquidity preferences reasonably tip the decision.

---

**3. Schwab US Dividend Equity ETF (SCHD) at ~3.50% trailing yield**

SCHD's distributions are historically 90–95% qualified dividends, taxed at LTCG rates. Using 93% qualified for conservatism; expense ratio 0.06%.

- Pre-tax yield net of expenses: 3.50% − 0.06% = 3.44%
- Qualified portion (93%): 3.44% × 0.93 = 3.20% → taxed at 20% combined = 0.64% tax
- Ordinary portion (7%): 3.44% × 0.07 = 0.24% → taxed at 37% combined = 0.09% tax
- Total tax drag: 0.73%
- **After-tax yield: 3.44% − 0.73% = 2.71%**

SCHD beats the HYSA after tax by 13 basis points — and that's before counting any price appreciation, which is not guaranteed but has historically accompanied the dividend growth mandate. The qualified-dividend treatment is the structural advantage; the gross yield looks lower than the HYSA but the after-tax result flips the ranking.

---

**4. JPMorgan Nasdaq Equity Premium Income Exchange-Traded Fund (JEPQ) at ~9.00% trailing yield**

JEPQ generates income primarily through Equity-Linked Notes (ELNs) used to implement its covered-call overlay. That income is classified as ordinary income, not qualified dividends — confirmed in JPMorgan's annual distribution disclosures. A small slice (~10–15%) may qualify, but using 10% qualified as a conservative estimate. Expense ratio: 0.35%.

- Pre-tax yield net of expenses: 9.00% − 0.35% = 8.65%
- Ordinary portion (90%): 8.65% × 0.90 = 7.79% → taxed at 37% = 2.88% tax
- Qualified portion (10%): 8.65% × 0.10 = 0.87% → taxed at 20% = 0.17% tax
- Total tax drag: 3.05%
- **After-tax yield: 8.65% − 3.05% = 5.60%**

This is the highest after-tax yield in the group — but the gap between gross and net is brutal: 9.00% headline becomes 5.60% in hand, a 38% haircut. Hold JEPQ in a taxable account and you're converting what could be deferred capital appreciation into a fully-taxable annual income stream. The instrument is purpose-built for tax-advantaged accounts; in a Roth Individual Retirement Account (IRA), the 9% gross yield is the yield you keep.

**The ranking:**

| Instrument | Gross Yield | After-Tax Yield (32% fed / 5% state) |
|---|---|---|
| JEPQ | 9.00% | **5.60%** |
| 10-Year Treasury | 4.47% | **3.04%** |
| SCHD | 3.50% | **2.71%** |
| HYSA | 4.10% | **2.58%** |

**What's shifted materially:** Six months ago, when the top HYSA was paying 4.6–4.8% APY, the HYSA beat SCHD after tax by 15–30 basis points. At 4.10% APY, that advantage has evaporated — SCHD now leads by 13 basis points and retains equity upside the HYSA never had. The window where cash simply dominated dividend equities on an after-tax basis has closed for most 32%+ bracket investors.

The practical allocation implication: JEPQ belongs in tax-advantaged space, period. SCHD's qualified-dividend treatment makes it competitive in taxable accounts despite a lower headline yield. Treasuries remain the cleanest high-quality fixed-income option for high-state-tax residents once the state exemption is factored in.

## Three Reads

- [How confident is it that stocks should outperform bonds over a 40+ year period?](https://www.bogleheads.org/forum/viewtopic.php?p=8781642#p8781642) — The thread correctly identifies that catastrophic scenarios where bonds outperform stocks are exactly the scenarios where the magnitude of loss on equities matters most; worth reading as a stress-test on long-horizon equity assumptions, not a reason to de-risk.

- [TSP G Fund rate 4.125%](https://www.bogleheads.org/forum/viewtopic.php?p=8781621#p8781621) — The G Fund's 4.125% with principal protection and no mark-to-market risk is legitimately hard to replicate outside of federal employment; the negative-real-return critique applies to the last decade but not the current rate environment.

- [Total Portfolio Allocation and Withdrawal (TPAW)](https://www.bogleheads.org/forum/viewtopic.php?p=8781648#p8781648) — TPAW's framework distinguishes between human-capital-funded future contributions and current portfolio, which is why it outputs seemingly low equity allocations for younger accumulators; understanding the input assumptions matters before rejecting the output.
