## The Move
### The Mega Backdoor Roth: How Much You Can Actually Stuff In (And What It's Worth)

The number most people cite is wrong. The 2026 Internal Revenue Service (IRS) Internal Revenue Code (IRC) Section 402(g) elective deferral limit is $23,500 (or $31,000 if you're 50+). That's what fills most conversations about 401(k) contributions. The more interesting number is $70,000 — the 2026 IRC Section 415(c) total annual additions limit for a participant under age 50.

The gap between those two figures is where the mega backdoor Roth lives.

**The math on available after-tax room:**

- Section 415(c) total limit: **$70,000**
- Employee pre-tax/Roth deferral: **$23,500**
- Employer match (assume 5% of $150K W-2 salary): **$7,500**
- After-tax contribution room: **$70,000 − $23,500 − $7,500 = $39,000**

At a higher salary — say $200K with a 5% match — the employer match rises to $10,000, leaving **$36,500** in after-tax room. At a salary above $280K where the match exceeds $23,000, the available window compresses further, but the floor for most high earners with a standard employer match lands in the **$36,000–$46,500 range**.

For this analysis, use a round **$39,000** as the after-tax contribution. The plan must explicitly allow after-tax contributions (non-Roth) and either an in-plan Roth conversion or an in-service distribution to a Roth Individual Retirement Account (IRA). The conversion mechanic matters: convert immediately after each contribution and you pay tax only on any minimal earnings accrued between contribution and conversion — effectively zero if you convert within days. Delay the conversion and ordinary income tax applies to all accumulated earnings.

**Lifetime impact: Roth IRA vs. taxable brokerage, 30-year horizon**

Assumptions: $39,000/year contributed for 10 years starting at age 35, then left to compound through age 65. 7% real annual return throughout. Federal marginal rate 32% at contribution, 23.8% blended rate on taxable returns (the 20% Long-Term Capital Gains (LTCG) rate plus 3.8% Net Investment Income Tax (NIIT) applicable at this income level).

*Mega backdoor Roth path:*

Contributions are after-tax — no deduction was claimed — so the conversion carries no additional tax cost when done immediately. The full $39,000/year enters the Roth and grows tax-free.

- FV of 10 annual contributions of $39,000 at 7%, grown for 30 years total (last contribution at year 10 grows for 20 years, first for 30): this is an annuity-due calculation.
- Future value of annuity at end of year 10: $39,000 × [(1.07^10 − 1) / 0.07] = $39,000 × 13.816 = **$538,824**
- That balance then compounds for another 20 years: $538,824 × 1.07^20 = $538,824 × 3.8697 = **$2,085,000** (terminal Roth balance, age 65)
- Tax at withdrawal: **$0**. Terminal after-tax value: **$2,085,000**.

*Taxable brokerage path (same $39,000/year after-tax dollars):*

The taxable account earns 7% gross, but an annual tax drag applies. A reasonable estimate for a buy-and-hold index fund position is approximately 0.5% per year in annual tax drag from dividends and any incidental distributions — net real return roughly 6.5% during accumulation. On the terminal liquidation, remaining unrealized gains are taxed at 23.8%.

- FV of annuity at 7% gross but compounding at 6.5% net during accumulation: $39,000 × [(1.065^10 − 1) / 0.065] = $39,000 × 13.494 = **$526,269**
- Compound for 20 more years at 6.5%: $526,269 × 1.065^20 = $526,269 × 3.5236 = **$1,854,900** gross terminal value
- Cost basis: $390,000 (10 × $39,000). Gain: $1,464,900. Tax on liquidation at 23.8%: $348,646.
- Terminal after-tax value: $1,854,900 − $348,646 = **$1,506,254**

**The differential: $2,085,000 − $1,506,254 = $578,746 in additional terminal after-tax wealth** from using the mega backdoor Roth structure over 10 years of contributions, with no change in the dollars invested or the gross return.

That $578K gap is purely structural — it buys nothing except avoidance of taxes that would otherwise compound against you for three decades.

**Plan eligibility is the real constraint.** The mega backdoor Roth requires three things your plan may not have: (1) explicit allowance of after-tax (non-Roth) employee contributions, (2) either in-plan Roth conversion language or in-service distribution rights, and (3) no nondiscrimination testing failure that would cap highly compensated employee contributions. Solo 401(k) holders at Fidelity and Vanguard can typically structure this themselves. Participants in large corporate plans need to check their Summary Plan Description — specifically the section on "after-tax contributions" and "in-service withdrawals."

If the plan allows it and you're not doing it, the opportunity cost is now quantified.
