## The Move
### Roth Conversion Wins at 32%—and It's Not Close After the Tax Cuts and Jobs Act Sunset

The standard Roth-versus-Traditional Individual Retirement Account (IRA) debate gets muddied by hand-waving about future tax rates. This week: no hand-waving. Terminal after-tax values, three brackets, one set of assumptions, and a Tax Cuts and Jobs Act (TCJA) sunset overlay that materially changes the answer.

**Assumptions (held constant across all scenarios)**
- Principal: $200,000 in a Traditional IRA
- Horizon: 18 years to Required Minimum Distribution (RMD) age
- Nominal return: 7% annually
- Conversion tax paid from outside the IRA (cash or taxable account), not from IRA principal — the only structurally honest comparison
- RMD-era withdrawal taxed as ordinary income at the same bracket as conversion (pre-sunset scenarios)

**The core formula**
- Roth terminal value = (Principal − Conversion Tax Paid) × (1.07)^18, then withdrawn tax-free
- Traditional terminal value = Principal × (1.07)^18 × (1 − RMD tax rate)

Note: Conversion tax is paid from outside funds, so the full $200,000 compounds inside the Roth. The outside-funds cost is real — at 5.05% (10-year Treasury yield), that cash has a measurable alternative use — but since both scenarios invest the same $200K inside the account, the comparison is apples-to-apples on the compounding side.

**(1.07)^18 = 3.3799**

Traditional terminal (gross): $200,000 × 3.3799 = **$675,980**

---

**Scenario A: 24% bracket (conversion rate = RMD rate)**

- Conversion tax paid outside: $200,000 × 0.24 = $48,000
- Roth terminal: $200,000 × 3.3799 = **$675,980** (tax-free)
- Traditional terminal: $675,980 × (1 − 0.24) = **$513,745**
- **Roth advantage: +$162,235**

Wait — both scenarios look identical on the compounding math, and yet Roth wins by $162K? Correct. Because the outside-funds conversion tax payment means the full $200K compounds in both cases. The Roth version withdrew at 0%; the Traditional version surrenders 24% at withdrawal. If your conversion rate equals your future RMD rate, Roth wins every time when tax is paid from outside funds. The bracket itself doesn't determine the winner — the payment source does.

---

**Scenario B: 32% bracket (conversion rate = RMD rate)**

- Conversion tax paid outside: $200,000 × 0.32 = $64,000
- Roth terminal: $200,000 × 3.3799 = **$675,980** (tax-free)
- Traditional terminal: $675,980 × (1 − 0.32) = **$459,666**
- **Roth advantage: +$216,314**

The Roth advantage grows with the bracket because the Traditional terminal value shrinks as the RMD tax bite increases. At 32%, the outside-funds cost ($64,000) is substantial, but the 18-year compound benefit is unambiguous.

---

**Scenario C: 35% bracket (conversion rate = RMD rate)**

- Conversion tax paid outside: $200,000 × 0.35 = $70,000
- Roth terminal: $200,000 × 3.3799 = **$675,980** (tax-free)
- Traditional terminal: $675,980 × (1 − 0.35) = **$439,387**
- **Roth advantage: +$236,593**

---

**Summary table (pre-sunset)**

| Bracket | Roth Terminal | Traditional Terminal | Roth Advantage |
|---|---|---|---|
| 24% | $675,980 | $513,745 | +$162,235 |
| 32% | $675,980 | $459,666 | +$216,314 |
| 35% | $675,980 | $439,387 | +$236,593 |

The structural point: when tax is paid from outside funds, Roth is *always* superior if the conversion and withdrawal rates are equal. The strategic question is whether you can convert today at a *lower* rate than you'll face at RMD age. If TCJA sunsets, that question answers itself.

---

**TCJA Sunset Sensitivity**

TCJA's individual rate provisions expire after 2025 under Internal Revenue Service (IRS) current law unless Congress acts. Under the pre-TCJA schedule, the 35% bracket reverts to 39.6% and the 32% bracket reverts to 35%. Re-running the same math with conversion at today's rate and RMD-era withdrawal at the post-sunset rate:

**32% conversion today → 35% at RMD (sunset)**
- Roth terminal: $675,980
- Traditional terminal: $675,980 × (1 − 0.35) = $439,387
- **Roth advantage: +$236,593** (vs. +$216,314 at flat rates)

**35% conversion today → 39.6% at RMD (sunset)**
- Roth terminal: $675,980
- Traditional terminal: $675,980 × (1 − 0.396) = $408,242
- **Roth advantage: +$267,738** (vs. +$236,593 at flat rates)

The sunset scenario adds $20,000–$31,000 to the Roth advantage depending on bracket. For anyone currently in the 32% or 35% bracket who has reason to expect higher rates at RMD age — whether from sunset, portfolio growth, Social Security income stacking, or reduced deductions in retirement — the terminal-value gap is not close.

**The answer to the core question:** Roth conversion beats deferral at every bracket when conversion tax is paid from outside funds and rates are stable or rising. The 32% bracket is the practical inflection point for most Financial Independence, Retire Early (FIRE) candidates: large enough balances to make the conversion meaningful, and a realistic sunset risk that makes future rate reduction implausible. If you're converting at 32% today and expect 35%+ at RMD age, the $236K+ advantage on $200K of principal is the arithmetic case — not an opinion.

## Closing

The most underused variable in Roth conversion analysis isn't the tax rate — it's whether the conversion tax comes from inside or outside the IRA, and almost every rule-of-thumb that says "conversion only makes sense if future rates are higher" is implicitly assuming the wrong payment source.
