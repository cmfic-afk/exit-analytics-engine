## The Move
### HSA Receipt Hoarding: Exactly What the Triple-Tax Advantage Is Worth

The Health Savings Account (HSA) triple-tax advantage — deductible contribution, tax-free growth, tax-free qualified withdrawal — is real. But the actual dollar value depends almost entirely on *when* you pull the trigger on reimbursement. The decision to pay medical bills out-of-pocket today and leave HSA funds invested is not a vague "maximize growth" heuristic. It's a calculable bet with a specific breakeven.

**The Setup**

Two scenarios, both starting with a 35-year-old in the 32% federal marginal bracket contributing $4,300 (2026 single HSA limit) this year:

- **Scenario A (Spend Now):** Use the $4,300 HSA contribution to reimburse $4,300 of current medical expenses immediately. The HSA account holds $0 after withdrawal. The $4,300 that would have been paid out-of-pocket is instead invested in a taxable account in an 80/20 portfolio at 7% nominal return. Dividends and gains taxed at blended 15% effective rate annually (conservative assumption for a high earner using broad index funds).

- **Scenario B (Invest and Hoard Receipts):** Pay the $4,300 medical bill with after-tax dollars — costing $6,324 pre-tax at 32% bracket ($4,300 / 0.68). The HSA $4,300 stays invested at 7% in an 80/20 portfolio (think Vanguard LifeStrategy 80 or equivalent, ~0.11% expense ratio, negligible drag). Receipts are saved. Reimbursement claimed at the comparison age, tax-free under Internal Revenue Service (IRS) Notice 2004-50, which permits reimbursement of qualified expenses incurred in any prior year as long as the HSA existed at the time of the expense.

**The Terminal Value Math**

HSA grows tax-free at 7%. Taxable account grows at approximately 6.1% after a blended annual tax drag (7% gross, minus ~0.9% annual friction on dividends and realized gains — consistent with a buy-and-hold index investor at the 15% Adjusted Gross Income (AGI) rate).

*HSA $4,300 at 7%, compounded:*
- Age 65 (30 years): $4,300 × (1.07)^30 = $4,300 × 7.612 = **$32,733**
- Age 70 (35 years): $4,300 × (1.07)^35 = $4,300 × 10.677 = **$45,911**
- Age 80 (45 years): $4,300 × (1.07)^45 = $4,300 × 21.002 = **$90,309**

All three figures are fully tax-free on withdrawal for qualified medical expenses — no federal income tax, no Required Minimum Distribution (RMD) pressure.

*Taxable account: after-tax cost is $4,300 invested (the out-of-pocket payment itself), but the true opportunity cost is the $6,324 pre-tax equivalent. However, to keep scenarios comparable — both start with $4,300 of actual dollars deployed — the taxable account starts at $4,300 at 6.1% net:*
- Age 65: $4,300 × (1.061)^30 = $4,300 × 5.991 = **$25,762** (then taxed at 15% Long-Term Capital Gains (LTCG) on the gain: basis $4,300, gain $21,462, LTCG tax $3,219 → **after-tax: $22,543**)
- Age 70: $4,300 × (1.061)^35 = $4,300 × 8.011 = **$34,447** → LTCG on $30,147 gain = $4,522 → **after-tax: $29,925**
- Age 80: $4,300 × (1.061)^45 = $4,300 × 14.329 = **$61,615** → LTCG on $57,315 gain = $8,597 → **after-tax: $53,018**

**The Value Differential (Scenario B minus Scenario A, after-tax)**

| Age | HSA (tax-free) | Taxable (after LTCG) | Advantage of Hoarding |
|-----|----------------|----------------------|-----------------------|
| 65 | $32,733 | $22,543 | **+$10,190** |
| 70 | $45,911 | $29,925 | **+$15,986** |
| 80 | $90,309 | $53,018 | **+$37,291** |

That's the actual dollar value of saving a single year's medical receipts and deferring reimbursement — $10K to $37K depending on how long you wait, on one year's $4,300 contribution. Stack 20 years of contributions and receipts and the compounding becomes the dominant variable in a high-earner's retirement asset base.

**The Post-65 Nuance**

After age 65, HSA dollars spent on *non-medical* purposes are taxed at ordinary income rates — identical to a Traditional Individual Retirement Account (IRA). The triple-tax advantage only survives for qualified medical expenses. This matters because most retirees have substantial healthcare costs anyway: Fidelity's 2025 retiree healthcare estimate put a 65-year-old couple at $330,000 in lifetime expenses, meaning the qualified-expense runway is long for most readers.

**The Practical Requirement**

This strategy works under IRS Notice 2004-50 and has survived multiple review cycles without legislative restriction. The mechanics: keep digital copies of every Explanation of Benefits, out-of-pocket receipt, and HSA statement in a durable location (a dedicated folder in cloud storage, timestamped). The IRS has never set a deadline on reimbursement timing — only that the expense must have been incurred after the HSA was established. If that changes legislatively, you can always reimburse immediately; the optionality costs nothing to maintain.

**The actual breakeven question:** paying out-of-pocket and hoarding receipts is better than spending the HSA immediately from day one, assuming any investment horizon longer than roughly 3 years at a 32% bracket (the tax shield on the HSA going in more than covers 3 years of growth differential). The strategy dominates at every horizon shown above — the question is only *how much* better it gets over time.
