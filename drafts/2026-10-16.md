## The Move
### The Savings Rate That Actually Gets You to FI in 12 Years (With Current Real Yields)

Mr. Money Mustache's shockingly simple math maps savings rates to years-to-FI using two fixed assumptions: 5% real return on investments, and a 4% safe withdrawal rate. At a 60% savings rate, you reach FI in roughly 12.5 years. At 65%, you split the difference and hit approximately 11 years. This framework has been cited thousands of times — but it was built for a generic assumed real return, not a specific rate environment.

Right now, we can be more precise.

**Step 1: Current real yield**

10-year Treasury nominal yield: 5.19%
Core CPI Year-over-Year (YoY): 2.6%
Real yield: 5.19% − 2.6% = **2.59%**

A diversified equity portfolio isn't a 10-year Treasury, but this real yield establishes a hard floor. A 60/40 blended portfolio in this environment — with equities historically delivering 4–5% real and fixed income now delivering 2.59% real — plausibly delivers **3.8–4.2% real** in aggregate. For this analysis, we'll use **4.0% real** as the revised baseline: more defensible than the standard 5%, and grounded in current observable yields rather than long-run equity averages.

**Step 2: The FV-based years-to-FI formula**

The underlying logic: if you spend $X per year and save $Y per year, your FI number is $X / 0.04 (the 4% withdrawal rate rule). You reach it when your portfolio grows to that level.

Let savings rate = s. Then spend rate = (1 − s). FI target as a multiple of annual spending = 1 / 0.04 = **25×**.

Years to FI = log(25 × (1 − s) / (s / r × ((1 + r)^n − 1))) solved for n — or more practically, we can use the annuity future value formula:

FV of savings after n years = (Annual Savings) × [((1 + r)^n − 1) / r]

We need FV = FI target. Normalizing annual income to $1:
- Annual savings = s
- Annual spending = (1 − s)
- FI target = (1 − s) / 0.04 = 25(1 − s)

Set equal:
s × [((1 + r)^n − 1) / r] = 25(1 − s)

Solve for n:
((1 + r)^n − 1) / r = 25(1 − s) / s
(1 + r)^n = 1 + 25r(1 − s) / s
n = log(1 + 25r(1 − s) / s) / log(1 + r)

**Step 3: Solve at r = 5% (standard) for calibration**

At s = 0.60, r = 0.05:
n = log(1 + 25 × 0.05 × 0.40 / 0.60) / log(1.05)
n = log(1 + 0.8333) / log(1.05)
n = log(1.8333) / 0.04879
n = 0.6061 / 0.04879 = **12.4 years** ✓

This matches the standard table. Calibration confirmed.

**Step 4: Solve at r = 4.0% (current real-yield-adjusted) for n = 12**

Now flip it: what savings rate s produces exactly 12 years at 4.0% real?

Plugging n = 12, r = 0.04:
(1.04)^12 = 1.6010
[((1.04)^12 − 1) / 0.04] = [0.6010 / 0.04] = 15.026

We need: s × 15.026 = 25(1 − s)
15.026s = 25 − 25s
40.026s = 25
s = 25 / 40.026 = **0.6246, or approximately 62.5%**

At the standard 5% real return assumption, a 60% savings rate reaches FI in 12.5 years. At the more conservative 4% real return (warranted by current yield conditions), reaching FI in exactly 12 years requires a **62.5% savings rate** — 2.5 percentage points higher.

**What that means in dollars**

For a household earning $300,000 after-tax: the difference between 60% ($180,000/year saved) and 62.5% ($187,500/year saved) is $7,500/year — roughly maxing out one additional Individual Retirement Account (IRA) and a Health Savings Account (HSA) contribution annually. Not a dramatic adjustment, but a real one.

**Caveats baked in**

This assumes a constant savings rate year-over-year (no income growth, no windfalls, no sequence-of-returns variance). Income growth would lower the required rate; a market drawdown in years 10–12 would raise it. The 4% withdrawal rate itself carries sequence risk that this formula doesn't model. And this analysis starts from zero — any existing portfolio balance compresses the timeline meaningfully.

The headline: the 60%-gets-you-there-in-12-years rule is slightly optimistic under current real-return conditions. **62.5% is the corrected target.** If you're already at 65%+, you're ahead of both versions of the math.
