## The Move
### The Roth Conversion Breakeven Isn't Where Most People Think It Is

The standard framing — "convert if your future tax rate will be higher" — glosses over the math that actually determines the outcome. Terminal after-tax value comparison does it cleanly and removes the ambiguity.

**The framework:**
- Roth terminal value = (Principal − Conversion Tax Paid) × (1 + r)^n
- Traditional Individual Retirement Account (IRA) terminal value = Principal × (1 + r)^n × (1 − Tax Rate at Required Minimum Distribution (RMD))

Critical assumption: conversion tax is paid from *outside* the IRA. If you fund the tax bill from the IRA itself, you're reducing the compounding base — that's a separate, worse scenario not being modeled here.

**Base case assumptions:** $200,000 principal, 18-year horizon to RMD age, 7% nominal return, conversion tax paid from a taxable account.

Growth factor: (1.07)^18 = **3.3799**

---

**Scenario A: 24% bracket at conversion, 24% at RMD**

- Roth: ($200,000 − $48,000) × 3.3799 = $152,000 × 3.3799 = **$513,745**
- Traditional: $200,000 × 3.3799 × (1 − 0.24) = $675,980 × 0.76 = **$513,745**

Result: **Dead even.** When conversion rate equals RMD rate, the two approaches are mathematically identical — which is what the algebra predicts. This is the baseline from which everything else is a deviation.

---

**Scenario B: 32% bracket at conversion, 32% at RMD**

- Roth: ($200,000 − $64,000) × 3.3799 = $136,000 × 3.3799 = **$459,667**
- Traditional: $200,000 × 3.3799 × (1 − 0.32) = $675,980 × 0.68 = **$459,667**

Result: **Still dead even** — same bracket both periods, same outcome. The Roth math only *breaks from* the Traditional when the rates diverge.

---

**Scenario C: 32% bracket at conversion, 35% at RMD (the realistic FatFIRE case)**

This is the scenario worth running. A dual-income household converting at 32% today faces RMD-era income — Social Security, pension, portfolio distributions — that pushes them into 35%.

- Roth: $136,000 × 3.3799 = **$459,667**
- Traditional: $675,980 × (1 − 0.35) = $675,980 × 0.65 = **$439,387**

**Roth wins by $20,280.** That gap represents roughly 4.4% more terminal wealth from a single conversion decision.

---

**Scenario D: 35% bracket at conversion, 35% at RMD**

- Roth: ($200,000 − $70,000) × 3.3799 = $130,000 × 3.3799 = **$439,387**
- Traditional: $675,980 × 0.65 = **$439,387**

Result: **Dead even again** — converting at 35% when RMDs will also be taxed at 35% produces zero net benefit before considering the opportunity cost of deploying those outside-IRA tax dollars elsewhere.

---

**Tax Cuts and Jobs Act (TCJA) Sunset Sensitivity**

Current brackets expire after December 31, 2025 under IRC §1 as modified by the Tax Cuts and Jobs Act (TCJA) — though Congress extended them in 2025's reconciliation bill through 2028. Planning to 2044 (18-year horizon) still carries meaningful sunset risk. Re-run assuming RMD-era brackets revert:

*35% converts to 39.6% at RMD:*
- Roth (converted at 35%): **$439,387** (unchanged — tax-free growth)
- Traditional: $675,980 × (1 − 0.396) = $675,980 × 0.604 = **$408,292**

**Roth wins by $31,095** — a 7.1% terminal wealth advantage. Converting at 35% looks like a wash under current law but looks like a clear winner if brackets revert.

*32% converts to 35% at RMD (already shown above): Roth wins by $20,280.*
*32% converts to 39.6% at RMD:*
- Roth: **$459,667**
- Traditional: $675,980 × 0.604 = **$408,292**

**Roth wins by $51,375** — the largest gap in the table. Converting at 32% with a 39.6% RMD scenario produces an 11.2% terminal wealth premium.

---

**The conclusion:**

If your RMD-era bracket will equal your conversion bracket, convert only for other reasons (estate planning, Roth Internal Revenue Service (IRS) rules eliminating RMDs under IRC §408A, heirs in high brackets). If you're currently at 32% and expect RMDs to push you to 35% or above — especially with a TCJA sunset in the mix — the conversion math is unambiguously positive. The 10-year yield at 4.68% matters here as a stress-test: if you discount the future tax savings at 4.68% to present value, the $20,280 gap in Scenario C has a PV of roughly $8,400. Even discounted, it clears zero.

The bracket where conversion clearly beats deferral: **32% in, 35%+ out.** At 35% in and 35% out, the case is sunset risk only — real, but not structural.
