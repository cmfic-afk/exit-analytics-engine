## The Move
### I-Bond vs. 3-Month Treasury: What the Spread Actually Says Right Now

The current Series I Savings Bond composite rate is approximately **3.98%** — a fixed component of 1.30% plus an inflation component tied to the September 2026 Consumer Price Index (CPI) adjustment. That composite sits 22 basis points *below* the 3-month Treasury at 4.20%. On the surface, Treasuries win. The case for I-Bonds is subtler and depends entirely on hold period and marginal tax rate.

**The raw rate comparison**

- 3-month Treasury: 4.20% annualized, fully liquid in secondary market, exempt from state and local income tax, federally taxable in the year interest is received.
- I-Bond composite: ~3.98% (fixed 1.30% + inflation ~2.68% CPI-based), $10,000/year per-individual purchase cap, 1-year hard lockup, 3-month interest penalty if redeemed before 5 years, federal tax deferred until redemption, state/local tax exempt.

**After-tax math at a 35% federal marginal rate, no state income tax**

For $10,000 invested, 1-year hold:

*3-month Treasury (rolling four quarters):*
Gross interest = $10,000 × 4.20% = $420
Federal tax = $420 × 35% = $147
Net = **$273**
After-tax yield = **2.73%**

*I-Bond, 1-year hold (with 3-month penalty):*
The 1-year hold triggers the 3-month forfeiture, so you earn 9 months of interest:
Gross interest = $10,000 × 3.98% × (9/12) = $298.50
Federal tax at redemption = $298.50 × 35% = $104.48
Net = **$194.02**
After-tax yield equivalent = **1.94%**

At 1 year, the Treasury wins by 79 basis points after-tax. This is the case against reflexive I-Bond purchases in a 4%+ short-rate environment.

**Break-even: the 5-year full-term hold**

At 5 years, the I-Bond's tax deferral and fixed component compound the advantage. Assume the composite rate averages 3.98% across the hold (conservative — it will reset; we're holding the current rate flat for the comparison).

*3-month Treasury (rolling, taxed annually at 35%):*
After-tax annual yield = 4.20% × (1 − 0.35) = 2.73%
Terminal value of $10,000 over 5 years = $10,000 × (1.0273)^5 = **$11,444**

*I-Bond, 5-year hold (no penalty, deferred tax at redemption):*
Gross terminal value = $10,000 × (1.0398)^5 = $12,157
Federal tax at redemption = ($12,157 − $10,000) × 35% = $754.95
Net terminal value = **$11,402**

At 5 years with flat rates and no state income tax, the Treasury *still* edges the I-Bond by $42 — essentially a wash, but technically the Treasury wins.

**When the I-Bond actually wins**

Three conditions shift the calculus:

1. **State income tax exists.** Add a 5% state rate (e.g., California, New York). The Treasury's after-tax annual yield drops to 4.20% × (1 − 0.35 − 0.05) = 2.52%. Terminal value over 5 years = $10,000 × (1.0252)^5 = $11,333. The I-Bond's net terminal value is unchanged at $11,402 — now the I-Bond wins by $69 per $10,000.

2. **Inflation accelerates.** The I-Bond's fixed 1.30% component is permanent for the life of the bond. If CPI reaccelerates to 4%+, the composite resets above 5% while short-term Treasury yields may or may not follow. The I-Bond is the only instrument that mechanically guarantees a real return floor (1.30% real) with no reinvestment risk on that fixed component.

3. **Tax rates rise at withdrawal vs. purchase.** If you buy now at 37% and expect to redeem in retirement at 22%, the deferred-tax structure of the I-Bond is explicitly valuable. The math above assumes a flat 35%; model your own brackets.

**The current verdict**

In a no-state-tax scenario with stable inflation and a sub-5-year horizon: the 3-month Treasury at 4.20% beats the I-Bond. The penalty structure is punishing and the composite rate gap is small but real.

In a high-state-tax domicile, or for readers who want a permanent 1.30% real return floor as an inflation hedge in their cash sleeve, the $10,000 annual allocation still earns its place — particularly for anyone whose 5-year window overlaps with a lower-bracket retirement period. The I-Bond is not a trading instrument; it's a tax-deferred, inflation-indexed savings certificate. If that's what you need, the entry point math is roughly neutral. If you need liquidity or are in a zero-state-tax state, park the cash in Treasuries and revisit when the fixed component rises above 1.50%.
