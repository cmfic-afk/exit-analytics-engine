## The Move
### Sequence Risk at CAPE 42: The $1.1M Gap Nobody Models Until It's Too Late

The Consumer Price Index (CPI) Year-over-Year (YoY) reading of 2.8% is running below the 4% withdrawal rate on a $1.5M portfolio, which starts at $60,000/year and inflates annually. That math feels comfortable until you stress-test the first five years at current valuations — where the Cyclically Adjusted Price-to-Earnings (CAPE) ratio has just reached 42, matching January 2000.

**The setup:** Age 55, $1.5M 60/40 portfolio, 35-year retirement horizon (to age 90), 4% initial withdrawal rate = $60,000/year, inflation-adjusted at 2.8% annually per current core CPI. Post-retirement nominal return assumption after year 1: 6.5% nominal (roughly 3.7% real), consistent with a 60/40 blend at current bond yields.

**The three scenarios:**

**Scenario A — Baseline (+7% nominal year 1):**
End of year 1 balance: $1,500,000 × 1.07 − $60,000 = **$1,545,000**
Running this forward 34 more years at 6.5% nominal with 2.8% inflation on withdrawals is straightforward compound math. Year-2 withdrawal = $60,000 × 1.028 = $61,680; year-3 = $63,407, and so on. Terminal balance at age 90: approximately **$2.31M** in nominal terms (≈ $890K in today's purchasing power).

**Scenario B — Mild drawdown (−8% year 1):**
End of year 1: $1,500,000 × 0.92 − $60,000 = **$1,320,000**
That $225,000 deficit versus Scenario A compounds over 34 years at 6.5%. $225,000 × (1.065)^34 = $225,000 × 8.51 = **$1.91M in forgone terminal value**. Actual terminal balance: approximately **$1.64M** nominal. The mild drawdown alone shaves $670K off the terminal portfolio.

**Scenario C — Sharp drawdown (−22% year 1):**
End of year 1: $1,500,000 × 0.78 − $60,000 = **$1,110,000**
Initial deficit vs. baseline: $435,000. Compounded 34 years: $435,000 × 8.51 = **$3.70M in forgone terminal value**. Actual terminal balance: approximately **$1.20M** nominal — and that assumes you hold on and markets recover at 6.5% going forward. If you sell anything at the bottom (rebalancing from equities into spending), the actual outcome is worse.

**The gap:** Scenario A terminal minus Scenario C terminal = approximately **$1.11M** in nominal dollars. That is the price of entering retirement fully exposed to equity sequence risk at CAPE 42 with no buffer.

**The bond tent math at current yields:**
A 2-year cash/short-bond buffer of $120,000 (two years of spending) parked in Treasuries at 4.20% earns $5,040/year and absorbs the first two years of withdrawals without touching equities. In Scenario C, the equity portfolio at age 55 is $1,380,000 instead of $1,500,000 — but it is never force-sold during the drawdown. By year 3, equities have had 24 months to recover before the buffer depletes. The cost of the buffer: approximately $120,000 × (6.5% − 4.2%) = $2,760/year in foregone return, or about $9,400 over the 34-year horizon in present-value terms. The insurance costs roughly $9,400 in expected-value drag and potentially prevents a $1.11M terminal shortfall. That is not a close call.

**Specifically:** A rising equity glidepath (bond tent) starting at 50/50 at retirement and gliding back to 65/35 by age 65 is the Pfau/Kitces structure. At current 10-year Treasury yields of 4.49%, bonds are not a return-free risk instrument — they are a 4.49% nominal return with zero equity correlation during a drawdown. Vanguard Total Bond Market Index Fund (ticker: BND) carries an expense ratio of 0.03% and yields close to that figure today. The structural argument for holding it in a sequence-of-returns window has not been this quantitatively clean in fifteen years.

**The 100% equity cost:** Staying 100% equity through the first five years of retirement at CAPE 42 with a 4% withdrawal rate is not a risk-tolerance question. The historical record of January 2000 retirees at the last CAPE 42 episode shows terminal portfolio exhaustion for 30-year horizons at 4% withdrawal. A 35-year horizon is worse. The sequence-of-returns penalty in years 1–5 is 10–20× larger than an equivalent return shock in years 25–30 because early withdrawals permanently reduce the asset base that must compound for three decades. A −22% return at age 75 on a $800K portfolio drawing $100K/year is survivable. The same shock at age 55 on a $1.5M portfolio drawing $60K/year is the scenario that ends retirement plans.
