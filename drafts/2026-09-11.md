## The Move
### Mega Backdoor Roth: The Real Ceiling and the 30-Year Math

The number most people get wrong: $46,500 — not the $7,000 Individual Retirement Account (IRA) limit, not the $23,500 employee deferral. That's approximately how much after-tax space sits inside a generous 401(k) plan right now, and almost nobody fills it.

**The mechanics of the gap**

Internal Revenue Code Section 415(c) caps total annual additions to a defined-contribution plan at **$70,000 for 2026** (for employees under 50). That total includes three buckets:

- Employee pre-tax or Roth 401(k) deferrals: **$23,500**
- Employer match/profit-sharing: varies; assume **$10,000** for this example (a common 4% match on a $250K salary)
- After-tax employee contributions (the mega backdoor slot): **$70,000 − $23,500 − $10,000 = $36,500**

With a more generous employer — say $13,500 in match/profit-sharing — the after-tax slot shrinks to **$33,000**. With no employer contribution at all (solo 401(k) or a plan with no match), the ceiling hits **$46,500** in after-tax contributions. The point: the after-tax space is real and large regardless of scenario.

**The conversion mechanic**

After-tax 401(k) contributions are not Roth contributions — they sit in their own subaccount with a cost basis. Left alone, the *growth* on those contributions is taxable upon distribution. The strategy is to move them into Roth status before growth accumulates, using one of two paths your plan may permit:

1. **In-plan Roth rollover** (Internal Revenue Code Section 402A(c)(4)): The plan converts the after-tax balance to a designated Roth account within the 401(k). No distribution required.
2. **In-service distribution**: You pull the after-tax subaccount out while still employed and roll principal to a Roth IRA and any earnings to a traditional IRA (or pay tax on the earnings — they're typically minimal if you convert promptly).

Both paths require your plan documents to permit them. Fidelity, Vanguard, and most large-plan recordkeepers support at least one; check Summary Plan Description Section 6 or equivalent before assuming access.

**The 30-year terminal value calculation**

Assume a 35-year-old contributing **$33,000/year** in after-tax contributions (converted immediately to Roth) for 10 years, then letting that balance compound untouched to age 65. Real return: **7%** gross. For taxable comparison, assume the same $33,000/year invested in a low-cost index fund with a 7% gross return, 0.03% expense ratio (Vanguard Total Stock Market Index Fund equivalent), and annual tax drag of **0.5%** on dividends/distributions taxed at 23.8% (20% Long-Term Capital Gains (LTCG) + 3.8% Net Investment Income Tax (NIIT) for a high earner), plus deferred capital gains tax of 23.8% on the terminal balance.

*Roth path — terminal after-tax value at 65:*

- 10-year accumulation at 7%: $33,000 × [(1.07^10 − 1) / 0.07] = $33,000 × 13.816 = **$455,928** at age 45
- That balance compounds 20 more years: $455,928 × 1.07^20 = $455,928 × 3.8697 = **$1,764,214**
- Tax owed at distribution: **$0** (qualified Roth distributions)
- **Roth terminal after-tax value: $1,764,214**

*Taxable path — terminal after-tax value at 65:*

- Net annual return after expense ratio and dividend tax drag: 7.00% − 0.03% − 0.50% = **6.47%**
- 10-year accumulation at 6.47%: $33,000 × [(1.0647^10 − 1) / 0.0647] = $33,000 × 13.305 = **$439,065** at age 45
- 20-year compounding at 6.47%: $439,065 × 1.0647^20 = $439,065 × 3.5268 = **$1,548,633** pre-liquidation
- Cost basis after 10 years of $33K contributions: $330,000
- Capital gain on liquidation: $1,548,633 − $330,000 = $1,218,633
- Federal LTCG + NIIT at 23.8%: $1,218,633 × 0.238 = **$290,034**
- **Taxable terminal after-tax value: $1,548,633 − $290,034 = $1,258,599**

**The differential: $1,764,214 − $1,258,599 = $505,615** in additional after-tax wealth from the same gross contributions, attributable entirely to tax structure. That's not a rounding error — it's a second retirement account.

The only variable that compresses this gap: if your marginal income tax rate at 65 drops dramatically, a traditional 401(k) approach may close some of the difference. For high earners with Required Minimum Distributions (RMDs), pension income, or Social Security, that rate compression rarely materializes. Roth terminal value is certain; traditional terminal value is a function of future tax law.

One execution note: convert after-tax contributions to Roth immediately — within days, not months. Every dollar of earnings that accumulates in the after-tax subaccount before conversion is ordinary income on rollover, not capital gains. Frequency of conversion matters.
