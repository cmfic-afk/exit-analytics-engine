## The Move
### The Deliberate Overfund: Turning a 529 Into a Roth Runway

Most families treat 529 overfunding as a planning error to avoid. The Setting Every Community Up for Retirement Enhancement Act (SECURE Act) 2.0 reclassifies it as a feature — if you set the account up correctly and early enough.

**The mechanics (Internal Revenue Service code §529(c)(3)(E), effective 2024)**

Starting in 2024, unused 529 balances can roll to a Roth Individual Retirement Account (IRA) for the *beneficiary* subject to four hard constraints:

1. The 529 account must have been open for at least 15 years.
2. Annual conversions are capped at the Roth IRA contribution limit for that year ($7,000 in 2025–2026 for under-50).
3. Lifetime cap per beneficiary: $35,000.
4. The Roth IRA must be in the beneficiary's name — not the account owner's.

At $7,000/year, exhausting the $35,000 lifetime limit takes five full years (5 × $7,000 = $35,000). If the annual limit rises — historically it has tracked inflation roughly every two or three years — you could clear it in fewer, but plan for five.

**What $35,000 is actually worth**

A 25-year-old who converts the full $35,000 and lets it compound tax-free at 7% real for 40 years:

> $35,000 × (1.07)^40 = $35,000 × 14.974 = **$524,100**

That $524,100 exits at 65 with zero federal income tax. No Required Minimum Distributions (RMDs). No ordinary income stacking that pushes up Medicare premiums or Adjusted Gross Income (AGI) for Net Investment Income Tax (NIIT) purposes.

For comparison: the same $35,000 sitting in a taxable brokerage, compounding at 7% real and liquidated at a 15% long-term capital gains rate:

> Terminal value: $524,100
> Tax on gain: ($524,100 − $35,000) × 0.15 = $73,365
> After-tax: **$450,735**

The Roth conversion is worth roughly $73,000 more in terminal after-tax dollars than taxable — and that's before state taxes, before any NIIT exposure, and before the behavioral benefit of a locked-in tax-free account.

**The 15-year clock is the real constraint**

If you open a 529 at a child's birth, the account is eligible at year 15 — when your kid is a teenager, conversion-ready well before they finish college. If you open it at age 8 hoping to use this provision, you can't convert until the child is 23. Open early or the window doesn't work as planned.

Recent contributions within the prior 5 years are ineligible for rollover — so you can't manufacture eligibility by dumping $35,000 in the account the year before conversion begins.

**The deliberate overfund strategy**

High-income households with high confidence their child will pursue scholarships, military service, trade school, or no college at all have historically faced a binary choice: contribute conservatively and leave money on the table, or overfund and eat a 10% penalty plus income tax on earnings at withdrawal.

SECURE 2.0 changes that math. A family can overfund by $35,000–$50,000 above expected education costs and designate the excess as future Roth seed money for the child. The $35,000 hard cap means you don't need to overfund dramatically — just enough to ensure the conversion pool survives even if education costs land lower than projected.

**What this does NOT solve**

The $35,000 lifetime cap is not inflation-indexed in the current statute. It is a fixed number. In 20 years it will feel smaller. The annual rollover limit is tied to the Roth contribution limit (which does adjust), but the lifetime ceiling does not move without new legislation.

Also: the Roth IRA must belong to the beneficiary. You cannot roll a grandchild's 529 into your own Roth. This is a wealth transfer tool to the next generation, not a personal tax-arbitrage move for the account owner.

**The planning decision**

If you have a newborn or toddler and are funding a 529 anyway, contribute $35,000–$50,000 above your education cost estimate with explicit intent to use the conversion provision. The 15-year clock starts immediately. The cost of that overfund sitting in the account — invested in index funds at 0.03%–0.05% expense ratios — is essentially zero. The upside is $524,000 of tax-free compounding that otherwise wouldn't exist.

For families with teenagers already at year 12 or 13: start the clock now if you haven't. A modest contribution today buys eligibility in three years. The annual $7,000 conversion limit means even a small existing balance can anchor the strategy.
