## The Move
### The After-Tax Yield Ranking That Just Shifted

Gross yield is the wrong number. For a 32% federal bracket investor — a household somewhere in the $200K–$500K income range — the after-tax yield on four common income instruments produces a ranking that probably isn't what you'd expect. Let's run it.

**Assumptions**
- Federal marginal rate: 32%
- Long-Term Capital Gains (LTCG) / qualified dividend rate: 15%
- State income tax rate: 5% (approximate national average for a state that taxes wage and investment income; Treasuries are state-exempt under 31 U.S.C. § 3124)
- Net Investment Income Tax (NIIT) excluded for simplicity (kicks in at $200K/$250K MAGI; adds 3.8% on top for some readers — adjust accordingly)

---

**1. 10-Year Treasury at 4.65%**

Interest is federally taxable, state-exempt.

- Federal tax: 4.65% × 32% = 1.488%
- State tax: $0 (exempt)
- After-tax yield: **4.65% − 1.488% = 3.162%**

---

**2. Top High-Yield Savings Account (HYSA) at ~4.10% Annual Percentage Yield (APY)**

Fully taxable at federal and state rates. Interest income is ordinary income under IRC § 61.

- Federal tax: 4.10% × 32% = 1.312%
- State tax: 4.10% × 5% = 0.205%
- After-tax yield: **4.10% − 1.312% − 0.205% = 2.583%**

---

**3. Schwab US Dividend Equity ETF (SCHD) trailing yield ~3.5%, qualified dividends**

SCHD's distributions are overwhelmingly qualified dividends (historically 95%+), taxed at 15% federal under IRC § 1(h). Expense ratio: 0.06%.

For simplicity, treating 100% as qualified. State still taxes dividend income at ordinary rates in most states.

- Federal tax: 3.5% × 15% = 0.525%
- State tax: 3.5% × 5% = 0.175%
- After-tax yield: **3.5% − 0.525% − 0.175% = 2.800%**

---

**4. JPMorgan Nasdaq Equity Premium Income Exchange-Traded Fund (JEPQ) trailing yield ~9.0%, mostly ordinary income**

JPEQ's income comes primarily from Equity-Linked Notes (ELNs) used to implement its covered-call strategy. ELN income is classified as ordinary income, not qualified dividends — confirmed in JEPQ's annual distributions. Historically, 80–90% of distributions are ordinary income. Using 85% ordinary / 15% qualified as a working split. Expense ratio: 0.35%.

- Ordinary income portion: 9.0% × 85% = 7.65%
  - Federal tax: 7.65% × 32% = 2.448%
  - State tax: 7.65% × 5% = 0.383%
  - After-tax on ordinary portion: 7.65% − 2.448% − 0.383% = 4.819%

- Qualified portion: 9.0% × 15% = 1.35%
  - Federal tax: 1.35% × 15% = 0.203%
  - State tax: 1.35% × 5% = 0.068%
  - After-tax on qualified portion: 1.35% − 0.203% − 0.068% = 1.079%

- Combined after-tax yield: **4.819% + 1.079% = 5.898%**

---

**Ranked by after-tax yield:**

| Instrument | Gross Yield | After-Tax Yield | Effective Tax Rate |
|---|---|---|---|
| JEPQ | 9.00% | 5.90% | 34.5% |
| 10-Year Treasury | 4.65% | 3.16% | 32.0% |
| SCHD | 3.50% | 2.80% | 20.0% |
| HYSA | 4.10% | 2.58% | 37.0% |

The ranking holds a few surprises. JEPQ wins on raw after-tax yield — but it's doing so by paying out capital efficiency as current income, with no price appreciation embedded in that yield. SCHD's after-tax yield at 2.80% is competitive despite a lower gross yield because the qualified dividend rate does real work at 32% marginal. The HYSA — which looks the most conservative — actually carries the highest effective tax rate of the four at 37%, and it trails a 10-year Treasury by 58 basis points after tax despite a nearly identical gross yield. That spread has widened materially over the past 12 months as HYSA rates have drifted down from their 5.0%+ peaks while 10-year yields have stayed elevated.

**The HYSA-to-Treasury gap is the one that moved.** Twelve months ago, top HYSAs were at 5.0–5.2% and 10-year Treasuries were around 4.2–4.4%. The after-tax gap was roughly flat or slightly favored HYSAs. Now it's inverted by nearly 60 basis points. For a $500K cash position, that's $3,000/year in after-tax yield left on the table by sitting in a savings account instead of laddering into Treasuries.

**Where JEPQ fits:** The 5.90% after-tax yield is real, but it belongs in a tax-deferred account — Individual Retirement Account (IRA), 401(k) — wherever the ordinary income treatment is neutralized. Holding JEPQ in a taxable account at 32% bracket is an inefficient use of a high-income instrument. SCHD in taxable, JEPQ in tax-deferred is the cleaner pairing.
