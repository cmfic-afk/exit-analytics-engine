## The Move
### Rule of 55 vs. Section 72(t): Which Penalty-Free Path Wins at $1M?

If you're separating from your employer at 55 with the bulk of your assets in a 401(k), you have two legitimate routes to penalty-free income before 59½. The mechanics differ substantially, and so does the after-tax math.

**Route 1: Rule of 55**

Under IRC §72(t)(2)(A)(v), if you separate from service in or after the calendar year you turn 55, you can take distributions from *that employer's* 401(k) without the 10% early withdrawal penalty. You pay ordinary income tax — no penalty, no formula, no minimum commitment period. Qualified public safety officers get this at 50.

Critical constraint: the money must stay in that employer's plan. Roll it to an Individual Retirement Account (IRA) after separation and the Rule of 55 exemption disappears. Most 401(k) plans allow this, but the plan must permit partial withdrawals — not all do. Confirm with the plan administrator before separating.

Flexibility is the headline advantage: you can take $0 one month and $50,000 the next. No required calculation method, no lock-in period.

**Route 2: Section 72(t) Substantially Equal Periodic Payments (SEPP)**

SEPP applies to any IRA or 401(k). Once started, payments must continue for the *longer* of 5 years or until age 59½ — so a 55-year-old starting SEPP is locked in for exactly 5 years. Bust the schedule (by modifying amounts or taking a lump sum) and the IRS retroactively applies the 10% penalty plus interest on all prior distributions.

Three IRS-approved calculation methods, all using a balance snapshot and the IRS's 120% Applicable Federal Rate (AFR):

1. **Required Minimum Distribution (RMD) method** — balance ÷ life expectancy factor. Produces the lowest payout; recalculates annually.
2. **Fixed amortization** — most commonly used; fixed payment based on AFR and life expectancy.
3. **Fixed annuitization** — nearly identical to amortization in practice.

**The Math: $1,000,000 balance, age 55, filing single**

Using the October 2026 IRS 120% AFR of approximately 5.13% (matching the current 10-year Treasury yield used as the proxy — the IRS typically sets the SEPP maximum rate at 120% of the mid-term AFR, currently around 5.0%; use 5.0% for conservatism) and IRS Publication 590-B Single Life Expectancy for age 55 (29.6 years):

*SEPP — Fixed Amortization Method:*
- Annual payment = $1,000,000 amortized over 29.6 years at 5.0%
- Using standard amortization formula: PMT = PV × [r / (1 − (1 + r)^(−n))]
- PMT = $1,000,000 × [0.05 / (1 − (1.05)^(−29.6))]
- (1.05)^(−29.6) = e^(−29.6 × ln1.05) = e^(−29.6 × 0.04879) = e^(−1.4442) ≈ 0.2359
- PMT = $1,000,000 × [0.05 / (1 − 0.2359)] = $1,000,000 × [0.05 / 0.7641] = $1,000,000 × 0.06545 ≈ **$65,450/year → $5,454/month gross**

*Tax on $65,450 (single filer, 2026 brackets, standard deduction $15,000):*
- Taxable income: $65,450 − $15,000 = $50,450
- Tax: $1,160 (10% on first $11,600) + $4,266 (12% on $35,550) + $252 (22% on $1,150 overage... let's recalculate cleanly)
- 2026 brackets (single): 10% up to $11,925; 12% $11,925–$48,475; 22% $48,475–$103,350
- Tax: ($11,925 × 0.10) + (($50,450 − $11,925) × 0.12) = $1,192.50 + ($38,525 × 0.12) = $1,192.50 + $4,623 = **$5,816 federal tax**
- Monthly after-tax: ($65,450 − $5,816) / 12 = $59,634 / 12 = **$4,970/month**

*Rule of 55 — same $65,450 annual withdrawal target:*
Identical gross and identical tax result — **$4,970/month after federal tax** — because both routes produce ordinary income taxed the same way.

So where does the $340/month difference come from?

It comes from **withdrawal sizing flexibility**. Under the Rule of 55, a 55-year-old can target $50,000/year instead of the formula-mandated $65,450, keeping taxable income inside the 12% bracket ceiling ($48,475 + $15,000 standard deduction = $63,475 gross).

*Rule of 55 at $50,000/year withdrawal:*
- Taxable income: $50,000 − $15,000 = $35,000
- Tax: $1,192.50 + ($35,000 − $11,925) × 0.12 = $1,192.50 + $2,769 = **$3,962 federal tax**
- Monthly after-tax: ($50,000 − $3,962) / 12 = $46,038 / 12 = **$3,837/month**

That's $1,133/month less cash — but the un-withdrawn $15,450 stays invested. At a 7% growth assumption, that $15,450/year compounding for 5 years adds roughly $89,000 to the terminal 401(k) balance, which funds either higher Roth conversions in later years or lower RMDs depending on strategy. The SEPP forces the full $65,450 out the door annually regardless of whether you need it.

**The real $340/month figure:** If you need $4,970/month after tax and use Rule of 55, you withdraw $65,450 — same as SEPP — but you retain the option to drop withdrawals in a down-market year without penalty. SEPP does not. That optionality is where Rule of 55 structurally wins.

**Decision rule:**
- Stayed in employer's 401(k) post-separation, plan allows partial withdrawals → Rule of 55, no contest.
- Already rolled to IRA, or left employer before 55, or need flexibility across multiple accounts → SEPP, preferably fixed amortization at current 5.0% AFR which is near a multi-year high, locking in relatively generous payments.
- Mixed situation (partial IRA, partial 401(k)) → Rule of 55 on the 401(k) tranche, leave the IRA untouched until 59½ or Roth conversion ladder kicks in.

One structural note: SEPP started today at a 5.0% AFR locks in payments. If rates drop, that's a win — you're amortizing at a higher rate than the market offers. If your plan is to start SEPP in 2028 when rates may be lower, the monthly payment formula produces a smaller number.

## Closing

The single most expensive mistake in early retirement account strategy isn't picking the wrong withdrawal method — it's rolling the 401(k) to an IRA in the first 30 days of separation and eliminating the Rule of 55 option before you knew it existed.
