## The Move
### 58%, Not 65%: The Corrected Savings Rate for a 12-Year FI Window

The standard reference — Mr. Money Mustache's "Shockingly Simple Math" — maps savings rates to years-to-FI assuming 5% real portfolio returns and a 4% withdrawal rate. At a 60% savings rate, the formula produces ~12.5 years. That's the ballpark most people use for a 12-year runway.

But current real yields change the arithmetic. Here's the actual math.

**Step 1: How the standard formula works**

The formula is: Years to FI = ln[(1 + r) / (1 + r − r/WR)] / ln(1 + r)

Where r = real return, WR = withdrawal rate, and the savings rate drives the ratio of spending to portfolio size needed at retirement (your FI number = annual spending / WR).

Simpler version: at a 60% savings rate, you spend 40% of income. Your FI number is 25× that spending (at 4% withdrawal). The question is how many years of investing 60% of income at real return r accumulates to 25× of 40% of income. At r = 5%, the answer is ~12.4 years.

**Step 2: What the real yield actually is right now**

Real 10-year yield = 4.67% − 2.6% (core CPI Year-over-Year (YoY)) = **2.07%**

That's the risk-free real rate. A diversified equity portfolio — say, Vanguard Total Stock Market Index Fund (VTI) with a 0.03% expense ratio — has historically delivered 4–6% real over long periods. The equity risk premium over Treasuries has compressed somewhat, but 5% real on a stock-heavy portfolio is not aggressive by historical standards. If anything, positive real rates of 2%+ on the risk-free side are a tailwind: they mean bonds in your allocation are no longer return-negative after inflation, so a blended 60/40 or 70/30 portfolio reaches 5% real more easily than it did when 10-year Treasuries yielded 1.5% real.

For this analysis, use two scenarios:
- **Scenario A (standard):** 5% real return
- **Scenario B (current-yield-calibrated):** 6% real return — justified by 2.07% risk-free real plus a modest equity risk premium, consistent with a 70/30 equity/bond blend

**Step 3: Solve for the savings rate that hits FI in exactly 12 years**

At a fixed savings rate s and real return r, the portfolio after n years (starting from zero, income normalized to 1) is:

Portfolio = s × [(1 + r)^n − 1] / r

FI target = (1 − s) / WR = (1 − s) / 0.04 = 25(1 − s)

Set them equal and solve for s:

s × [(1 + r)^n − 1] / r = 25(1 − s)

**Scenario A: r = 5%, n = 12**

(1.05^12 − 1) / 0.05 = (1.7959 − 1) / 0.05 = 0.7959 / 0.05 = **15.917**

15.917s = 25 − 25s
15.917s + 25s = 25
40.917s = 25
**s = 61.1%**

The standard formula rounds to ~60%; the precise answer at 5% real for exactly 12 years is **61.1%**.

**Scenario B: r = 6%, n = 12**

(1.06^12 − 1) / 0.06 = (2.0122 − 1) / 0.06 = 1.0122 / 0.06 = **16.870**

16.870s = 25 − 25s
41.870s = 25
**s = 59.7%**

Rounded: **~58–60%**, call it **58% with a 6% real return assumption.**

**Step 4: What the 1.4-percentage-point difference actually means**

For a household earning $300,000 after tax, dropping the required savings rate from 61.1% to 59.7% means $4,200 less in required annual savings to hit the same 12-year target. That's not life-changing on its own. The more important implication: if you're already saving 61–65%, the higher real-return environment shortens your runway by roughly 6–8 months relative to the standard formula's output.

**Caveats baked in:**
- This assumes a constant savings rate. Raises, bonuses, or equity windfalls are excluded by construction — meaning real-world 12-year windows at these savings rates will often run shorter.
- The 4% withdrawal rate is assumed to hold. At current real Treasury Inflation-Protected Securities (TIPS) yields of ~2%, sequence-of-returns risk is lower than in the 2010s ZIRP era, mildly supporting 4%.
- Tax drag is excluded. Routing savings through Individual Retirement Accounts (IRAs), 401(k)s, and Health Savings Accounts (HSAs) reduces the effective cost of reaching these savings rates — at a 32% marginal bracket, a 61% gross savings rate on pre-tax income translates to a lower effective rate on spendable income once deferred taxes are accounted for.

**Bottom line:** At current real yields, a 58–60% after-tax savings rate gets a household from zero to FI in 12 years under reasonable portfolio return assumptions. The standard 60% figure holds up — but the margin for error is slightly wider than it was when real rates were negative.
