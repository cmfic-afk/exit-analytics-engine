## The Move
### 7.11% Mortgage vs. 60/40: Running the Actual Numbers

The standard framing — "your mortgage rate is your guaranteed return on payoff" — is correct but incomplete. At 7.11%, most people assume investing wins easily. The math says the edge is narrower than the last decade trained us to expect, and for a specific slice of this readership it flips entirely.

**Step 1: What is the after-tax cost of the mortgage?**

The Tax Cuts and Jobs Act (TCJA) raised the standard deduction to levels that eliminated itemizing for roughly 90% of filers. If you're not itemizing — and at a $29,200 standard deduction (2024, married filing jointly; indexed upward each year) most dual-income households with a moderate mortgage balance aren't — mortgage interest provides zero marginal tax benefit. Your effective cost of the mortgage is the nominal rate: **7.11%**.

If you *are* itemizing (large mortgage balance, high property taxes, significant charitable giving), the after-tax rate scales with your marginal bracket. At the 32% bracket: 7.11% × (1 − 0.32) = **4.84%**. At 37%: 7.11% × (1 − 0.37) = **4.48%**. File that for the payoff threshold comparison below.

**Step 2: What does the 60/40 portfolio need to return to beat payoff?**

A 60/40 portfolio — 60% broad equities, 40% investment-grade bonds — has a reasonable long-run nominal return expectation of roughly 7.0–7.5% (equity ~9–10% nominal, bonds ~5% nominal at current yields, blended). Real return is approximately 4.5–5.0% assuming 2.5% inflation. That nominal figure sits *at or just below* the mortgage rate for non-itemizers.

For the portfolio to beat payoff after-tax on a 10-year horizon, it needs to clear 7.11% nominal annually — net of fund expenses, net of taxes on distributions in a taxable brokerage account.

A low-cost implementation (Vanguard Total Stock Market + bond fund, blended expense ratio ~0.05%) loses negligible ground to fees. But taxable drag matters. Assume 15% Long-Term Capital Gains (LTCG) tax on gains realized at the end of year 10, plus ~1.5% annual dividend yield on the equity sleeve taxed at 15% each year. Rough annual taxable drag: ~0.23% on a 60% equity position. Net expected nominal return: **~6.75–7.25%**. The midpoint is 7.0% — a 0.11% shortfall versus the mortgage rate for non-itemizers.

**Step 3: Terminal value comparison on $50,000, 10-year horizon**

*Mortgage payoff path:* $50,000 in extra principal eliminates interest at 7.11%/year compounded. Terminal value of that decision (interest saved, treated as guaranteed return): $50,000 × (1.0711)^10 = **$99,845**. This is riskless, fully after-tax, requires no sequence-of-returns tolerance.

*Brokerage investment path:* $50,000 compounding at 7.0% nominal (post-fee, pre-liquidation tax): $50,000 × (1.07)^10 = $98,358. Apply 15% LTCG on the $48,358 gain at liquidation: tax = $7,254. After-tax terminal value: **$91,104**.

At 7.5% nominal (optimistic scenario): $50,000 × (1.075)^10 = $101,364. LTCG on $51,364 gain = $7,705. After-tax terminal: **$93,659**.

The payoff wins in both scenarios for a non-itemizing household on a 10-year horizon.

**Step 4: When does investing flip back ahead?**

Three conditions shift the math:

1. **You're itemizing at 32%+.** Your effective mortgage cost drops to ~4.84%. A 60/40 portfolio at even 6.5% nominal clears that after LTCG drag on a 10-year horizon. Payoff's guaranteed return advantage evaporates.

2. **Longer horizon (20–30 years).** Equity compounding and the ability to defer LTCG until liquidation extends the portfolio's edge. At 20 years, $50,000 at 7.5% gross grows to $203,571 before tax vs. $50,000 at 7.11% compounded = $197,477. The tax-deferral benefit on a brokerage account narrows but doesn't close the gap — at 20 years the LTCG bill on $153,571 gain is $23,036, leaving **$180,535** vs. the payoff's **$197,477**. Payoff still wins at 20 years for the non-itemizer — but add a tax-advantaged wrapper (Roth Individual Retirement Account (IRA), 401(k)) and the picture reverses immediately, since there's no liquidation tax at all.

3. **The dollars going to payoff would otherwise go into a tax-advantaged account.** This is the biggest practical error people make. If the choice is extra mortgage principal vs. maxing a Roth IRA or a solo 401(k), the tax-advantaged account wins categorically — the guaranteed return on payoff doesn't escape taxes on future earnings the way a Roth does. Run payoff against *taxable* brokerage dollars only.

**The punchline:** At 7.11% with no itemized deduction benefit, paying down the mortgage beats a taxable 60/40 portfolio on a 10-year horizon by roughly $8,700 on a $50,000 decision — a ~9.6% advantage in terminal after-tax value. That margin shrinks materially at longer horizons and disappears entirely if the alternative is a tax-advantaged account. The correct sequencing: max all tax-advantaged space first, *then* run this comparison on the residual dollars.
