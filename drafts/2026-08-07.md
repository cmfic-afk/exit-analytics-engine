## The Move
### Rule of 55 vs. Section 72(t) SEPP: Which Exit Route Actually Costs Less?

The most commonly recycled piece of advice for early retirees with 401(k)-heavy portfolios is "use SEPP." It is often wrong. The correct answer depends on a single prior question: did you roll the 401(k) to an Individual Retirement Account (IRA) after separating?

**The Rule of 55 (IRC §72(t)(2)(A)(v))**

If you separate from service in the calendar year you turn 55 or later, withdrawals from *that employer's* 401(k) are exempt from the 10% early-distribution penalty. You do not need to roll anything. You do not lock into a payment schedule. You can take $0 one month and $50,000 the next. The flexibility is the feature. Age 50 applies for qualified public safety officers under IRC §72(t)(10).

Critical constraint: the money must stay in the 401(k). Rolling to an IRA after separation permanently kills the Rule of 55 exemption for those dollars. This is the single most expensive rollover mistake in early retirement.

**Section 72(t) Substantially Equal Periodic Payments (SEPP)**

SEPP applies to any IRA or 401(k) and has no age-55 floor. The Internal Revenue Service (IRS) permits three calculation methods:
1. **Required Minimum Distribution (RMD) method** — account balance ÷ life expectancy factor. Produces the lowest payment; recalculates annually.
2. **Fixed amortization** — amortizes balance over life expectancy at a rate not to exceed 120% of the mid-term Applicable Federal Rate (AFR). Produces the highest payment; fixed for the duration.
3. **Fixed annuitization** — uses an annuity factor. Payment falls between the other two; also fixed.

Once started, SEPP must run for the longer of 5 years or until age 59½. Modify or stop early and the IRS retroactively assesses the 10% penalty on every prior distribution, plus interest. That cliff is not theoretical — it catches people who inherit mid-stream, divorce, or hit a cash crunch.

**The Math: $1,000,000 Balance, Retiring at 55**

Assume: single filer, $1M 401(k), no other income, 2026 tax brackets, standard deduction of $15,000.

*Rule of 55 — target $60,000/year withdrawal:*
- Gross: $60,000
- Less standard deduction: $15,000
- Taxable income: $45,000
- Tax: 10% on $11,925 = $1,193 + 12% on $33,075 = $3,969 → total federal tax = $5,162
- After-tax annual income: **$54,838** → **$4,570/month**
- No penalty. Fully flexible. Can pause if a Roth conversion year is planned.

*SEPP — Fixed Amortization method, $1M balance, age 55:*
- 120% of the July 2026 mid-term AFR is approximately 5.40% (mid-term AFR ~4.50%, 120% = 5.40%).
- IRS life expectancy for single life at 55 using the Single Life Table: 31.6 years.
- Annual payment = $1,000,000 ÷ annuity factor. Using the present value of an annuity formula at 5.40% over 31.6 years: factor ≈ 15.38.
- Annual SEPP payment: $1,000,000 ÷ 15.38 = **$65,020/year**.
- This is not optional — you must take $65,020 every year regardless of need.
- Gross: $65,020. Taxable after standard deduction: $50,020.
- Tax: 10% on $11,925 = $1,193 + 12% on $38,095 = $4,571 → total federal tax = $5,764
- After-tax annual income: **$59,256** → **$4,938/month**

*RMD method SEPP, same balance:*
- Life expectancy factor at 55 from Uniform Lifetime Table (for RMD method IRS uses Single Life): 31.6
- Annual payment: $1,000,000 ÷ 31.6 = **$31,646/year** — well below what most early retirees need.
- After-tax: roughly $28,400/year after standard deduction and 10% bracket. Fine as a supplement; inadequate as a primary income stream.

**The $340/month gap** is between the Rule of 55 at $4,570/month (on a chosen $60K draw) and fixed amortization SEPP at $4,938/month — but the SEPP number is *mandatory*, not chosen. If your actual spending is $54,000/year, SEPP forces you to take $11,000 more than you need, accelerating tax drag and potentially triggering a higher bracket in future years when Roth conversion headroom matters most.

**Decision Framework**

| Scenario | Better choice |
|---|---|
| 401(k) still at former employer | Rule of 55 — no contest |
| Already rolled to IRA | SEPP (only option short of 59½) |
| Income varies year to year | Rule of 55 — flexibility premium is real |
| Want to run large Roth conversions in early years | Rule of 55 — dial withdrawals down in conversion years |
| Need income below the RMD method floor | SEPP fixed amortization or annuitization |

One final point the rollover decision usually obscures: if your 401(k) plan has poor investment options and high expense ratios, the flexibility of Rule of 55 has a cost. A plan charging 0.80% annually on $1M costs $8,000/year in drag versus a self-directed IRA holding Vanguard Total Stock Market Index Fund Admiral Shares at 0.04%. At that spread, SEPP from an IRA may produce better terminal after-tax wealth even accounting for the payment rigidity — run the terminal value comparison before deciding to stay put.
